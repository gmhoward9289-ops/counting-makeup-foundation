Where it came from

The fourth founding question — was it sourced ethically, and to which standard. The short answer is that an ingredient list cannot tell you, and the interesting part is why.

The question the label cannot answer

An ingredient declaration names what a substance is and never where it came from. That is a property of the labelling system, not a gap in this corpus — and it means no amount of care with FDA filings can tell you whether the glycerin in a particular bottle was responsibly sourced.

  • INCI names identify a substance chemically. Glycerin filed by one manufacturer and glycerin filed by another are the same INCI name whether the feedstock was palm, coconut, tallow or petrochemical synthesis.
  • The FDA-filed OTC drug label that this corpus is built from carries the same limit: it is a complete chemical declaration and a complete provenance blank.
  • So no amount of care with the corpus can answer an ethical-sourcing question about a specific bottle. That is a property of the labelling system, and it is the finding, not a gap in the research.

So this study does the only honest thing available: it maps where the sourcing question arises in these twelve formulas, and then goes to the company-level filings, which are the only primary documents that speak to provenance at all.

Where the question arises: palm

What a certification actually certifies

L'Oréal reports 92,260 tonnes of palm and palm derivatives for 2023, of which 99.5% of the certified volume is Mass Balance and 0.23% is physically separated from uncertified material. Certified, at this scale, is an accounting statement about volumes bought — not a claim that the molecule in your bottle is traceable to a certified plantation.

This is the corpus's largest parent by product count, and the figures come from its own filing to RSPO rather than from a sustainability brochure. Two things in it are worth reading closely. The first is that 99.8% of the volume is derivatives and fractions, not palm oil — in cosmetics, palm arrives as glycerin and stearates, which is exactly why it is invisible on a label. The second is the split by supply-chain model:

Identity Preserved and Segregated keep certified material physically separate. Mass Balance permits certified and uncertified material to be mixed in the supply chain, with volumes reconciled on paper. Book and Claim is a credit purchase with no physical link at all. A product whose maker reports mostly Mass Balance volume cannot be said to contain certified palm - only that the company bought as much certified volume as it used.

Source: L'Oréal RSPO Annual Communication of Progress 2023 — Roundtable on Sustainable Palm Oil. The company's own summary: “Since 2010, 100% of the palm oil purchased directly by L'Oréal has been certified sustainable according to the RSPO Segregated model. Since 2012, 100% of the palm and palm kernel derivatives used are RSPO certified, and in 2023 99.8% of derivative volumes were certified via the Mass Balance model.”

On the two percentages. The table above reports Mass Balance as 99.5% of all certified volume, across all four models. The company's statement reports 99.8% of derivative volume only, which excludes the crude palm oil that carries the Segregated certification. Both are correct and they are not the same denominator — the difference is the 209 tonnes of crude oil, out of 92,260.

Mica, and the one sourcing choice that is visible

Natural mica carries a child-labour concern in Indian and Madagascan mining. 2 of the 12 products list it among their colourants. 5 declare synthetic fluorphlogopite — mica grown in a reactor instead of dug out of the ground.

That substitution is one of the very few sourcing decisions a reader can actually see on an ingredient list. It is not necessarily made for ethical reasons — synthetic mica is also brighter and more uniform — which is precisely why the label cannot be read as a claim.

Talc appears in 1 product. Its sourcing question is asbestos contamination, because the two minerals occur together geologically. That is the one ingredient issue MoCRA addressed head-on, and the resulting rule was withdrawn.

What's banned where

The EU prohibits 1,760 substances in cosmetics. The United States prohibits nine. Testing that gap against twelve real American foundations gives an answer almost nobody expects.

The premise, tested

Not one of the 191 ingredients in this corpus is prohibited in the European Union. The EU's prohibited-substances list runs to 1,760 entries and the American one to 9, and that 196-fold gap does not separate these twelve products at all. Where the two regimes actually diverge is somewhere else entirely — in concentration ceilings, in which UV filters a regulator will approve, in a restriction that has not started yet, and in what has to be printed on the box.

The claim that American cosmetics are full of ingredients Europe has banned is common enough to be worth testing rather than repeating. It is testable here because the corpus is built from filings: every ingredient in every product is on the record, so the list can be run against the prohibition list directly.

How the match was done

Each of the 191 distinct INCI names in the corpus was normalised (lowercased, punctuation collapsed to single spaces) and tested against the normalised chemical-name column of all 1760 Annex II entries, matching on whole-string equality or whole-word containment. Every hit was then read by hand.

The raw comparison threw 9 string collisions. Every one turned out to be a longer chemical name that happens to contain a corpus ingredient's name inside it — which is exactly the kind of hit that a careless version of this study would publish as a finding.

Sunscreen: the divergence runs the other way

The EU permits 30 UV filters. The United States permits 17 — and reached that number ten weeks ago. On the one ingredient class where these two regimes differ most, the American regulator is the restrictive one.

Every product in this corpus carries an SPF claim, which is why its ingredients are filed with the FDA at all. The filters they use are the old ones. Bemotrizinol, permitted in Europe for years, was added to the US monograph by final order on 10 June 2026 at up to 6% — the first new American UV filter in decades.

And the older American filters are not settled either. Of the 16 filters already in the US monograph, only titanium dioxide and zinc oxide are affirmed as generally recognised as safe and effective. The other 14 - including octinoxate and octisalate, the filters this corpus actually runs on - remain in the monograph but their GRASE status is unresolved pending safety data.

Octinoxate, and a myth worth retiring

9 of the 13 products declare octinoxate. It is routinely described as banned in Europe. It is not: the EU permits it up to 10%, and the highest concentration anyone in this corpus files is 6.7%.

Local reef-protection laws in Hawaii, Key West and Palau restrict octinoxate in sunscreens, and those are real — but they are municipal and state measures, not an EU ban, and conflating the two is where the myth comes from.

The divergence that has not happened yet

3 of the 13 products contain a cyclic siloxane that the EU will bar from leave-on cosmetics after 6 June 2027 — 291 days from now. In the United States there is no restriction at all, and none in prospect.

A foundation is a leave-on product, so it falls under the later of the two dates in the restriction — a distinction press coverage regularly loses. The restriction is environmental rather than a consumer-safety finding: D4, D5 and D6 are classified as very persistent and very bioaccumulative.

What the label has to say
